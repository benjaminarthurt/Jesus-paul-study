# Augustine: grace, works and the reception of Paul

Historical reception study, draft. Augustine is a later interpreter, not evidence of the original intention of Jesus or Paul.

## Primary witnesses

[On Grace and Free Will](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1510.htm), chapters 2, 7, 18–20, 26, written 426 or 427: Augustine draws on Matthew 16:27 for accountability, Romans 3:28 and Galatians 5:6 for justification and active faith, James 2:19 against barren belief, and John 15:5 for dependence on grace. He treats righteous action and its reward as gifts of grace. Chapter 26 interprets the Lord's Prayer as evidence that divine help is needed to resist sin.

[On the Spirit and the Letter](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1502.htm), chapter 52: Augustine uses Romans 3:31 analogically to argue that grace restores rather than abolishes freedom, enabling love and fulfillment of the law.

[Letter 214](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1102214.htm), section 1: Augustine responds to disagreement among monks about whether grace eliminates free will and judgment according to deeds. He rejects that inference.

[On Nature and Grace](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1503.htm), chapter 4: Romans 3:24 supports his insistence that saving grace is not earned by antecedent merit.

## Historical significance

These selected passages demonstrate explicit reception of Gospel and Pauline texts in a later controversy over grace and agency. Augustine's synthesis is neither a simple rejection of Jesus' teachings about deeds nor an assertion that human merit independently earns salvation. His interpretive framework is a historically situated theological construction.

## Limits

Selected English translation excerpts actually retrieved; no Latin collation or critical edition review. The later influence on councils, medieval theology and Reformation confessions requires separate documentary evidence. Augustine treats Ephesians as Pauline; the project must preserve its modern authorship distinction when indexing original biblical witnesses.
