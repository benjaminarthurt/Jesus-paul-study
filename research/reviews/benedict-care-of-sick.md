# How the Rule of Benedict assigns care for sick members

Chapter 36 begins with two explicit appeals to Matthew 25. A sick person receives a visitor, and the king identifies himself with the recipient of that act. The Rule makes this a duty of the house. Someone must provide a room, appoint an attendant and take responsibility if care is neglected. The English version inspected addresses sisters and assigns oversight to the abbess. Those terms identify the form of this translation. They do not settle the wording of the original Latin. [Rule of Benedict, chapter 36](https://archive.osb.org/rb/text/rbemjo1.html) · [Matthew 25:34–40](/passages/mt-25-31-46/#unit-34-40-teaching)

The practical test comes when illness makes the usual discipline unsuitable. The chapter permits more frequent bathing and an exception to the ordinary diet for those who are very weak. Recovery changes how the dietary rule applies. It also asks sick members to consider the burden on their attendants and tells attendants to show patience. Ultimate responsibility remains with the head of the community. The rule therefore gives a member who needs care a claim on shared resources even when meeting that need requires changing a routine.

Matthew is directly invoked. Paul's image of members suffering together offers a comparison, but the inspected chapter does not cite him for these arrangements. The chapter is a prescription for a monastic household. It cannot by itself establish that a particular monastery followed it, that its remedies were effective, or that it caused later hospital practices. The English chapter was inspected on November 28, 2026. A Latin edition and independent evidence of implementation remain to be examined.
