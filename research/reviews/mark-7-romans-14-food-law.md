# Mark 7:14–23 and Romans 14:1–23 — food, defilement, and conscience

**Issue:** #3 · **Status:** preliminary draft; independent source verification pending

## Research question

Does Mark's presentation of Jesus on defilement establish a universal repeal of dietary distinctions, and is that equivalent to Paul's instruction about food and mutual judgment in Romans 14? These are separate questions, and neither passage should be reduced to a slogan.

## Primary-text comparison

| Passage | Literary observation | Qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mark 7:1–13 | The immediate dispute concerns handwashing traditions and obligations to parents. | A dispute over elders' tradition is not automatically a rejection of every Torah food distinction. |
| Mark 7:14–15 | Jesus contrasts what enters a person with what proceeds from within. | The precise scope of “defilement” requires context and interpretation. |
| Mark 7:18–19 | Jesus uses bodily digestion to explain his point; the Greek participial phrase commonly rendered “thus declaring all foods clean” has disputed syntax and speaker/narrator status. | Do not present the editorial gloss interpretation as an uncontested direct quotation of Jesus. |
| Mark 7:20–23 | Moral evils proceeding from the heart are emphasized. | This does not independently decide the status of every ritual practice. |
| Romans 14:1–6 | Paul addresses disputes about eating and observance of days, urging mutual nonjudgment. | The text does not clearly identify every food dispute as a Torah kosher-law controversy; ascetic practices may also be in view. |
| Romans 14:13–23 | Paul combines convictions about cleanness with a duty not to harm another's conscience; love and community peace govern practice. | Paul's statements should not be detached from their community-ethics argument. |

## Strongest tension reading

A reading of Mark 7:19 as an editorial statement that Jesus declared all foods clean, alongside Romans 14:14's conviction that nothing is unclean in itself, suggests substantial convergence between Mark's Jesus tradition and Paul on food purity. The tension then lies primarily with strong Torah-continuity readings of Matthew 5, not necessarily between these two passages.

## Strongest distinction reading

Mark's dispute begins with ritual handwashing and inner moral defilement; Romans 14 addresses communal tolerance among people with divergent eating convictions. Even where conclusions overlap, the texts differ in problem, audience, and reasoning. If Mark 7:19's participle belongs within the digestive explanation, the common “Jesus abolished kosher rules” claim becomes still less secure.

## Required counterchecks

- Matthew 15:1–20 presents a Synoptic parallel with distinct phrasing and focus.
- Romans 14:14 must be read with Romans 14:20–21, where Paul also prioritizes avoiding harm to others.
- Romans 3:31 and 7:12 prevent a blanket claim that Paul simply repudiated Torah.
- Acts 10–11 is a separate narrative witness; it should not be conflated with Paul's first-person position.
