# Matthew 5:17–20 and Galatians 3:19–29 — claim-level comparison

**Issue:** #3 · **Status:** preliminary research draft · **Classification:** apparent-tension (provisional)

This record supplements [the existing law-and-faith study](../comparisons/law-and-faith.md), rather than replacing it or promoting its status.

## Passage-level observations

| Passage | What the passage says in its literary context | What it does **not** settle alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matthew 5:17–18 | Matthew's Jesus denies coming to abolish the Law or Prophets and says fulfillment, not abolition, characterizes his mission; the enduring force of the Law is stressed. | Whether every Torah identity marker remains obligatory for Gentile Christ-followers. |
| Matthew 5:19–20 | Doing and teaching commandments matters, and righteousness is contrasted with that of scribes and Pharisees. | A complete list of commandments or how Matthew's community adjudicated later Gentile inclusion. |
| Galatians 3:19–22 | Paul asks why the Law was given, describes a role connected to transgressions and promise, and places it in a salvation-historical argument. | That Paul regarded the Law as evil or morally worthless. |
| Galatians 3:23–25 | Paul uses custodial/guardian imagery and describes a change with the coming of faith. | That all Jewish believers must abandon all ancestral observance. |
| Galatians 3:26–29 | Baptismal identity in Christ and belonging to Abraham's promise are central; Jew/Greek, enslaved/free, male/female distinctions are relativized in that claim. | That every social distinction vanished in practice or that every other Pauline instruction is automatically overridden. |

## Strongest tension reading

If Matthew 5:19 is construed as prescribing continued observance of *each* Mosaic commandment for *every* disciple, and Galatians 3:23–25 as ending that obligation for *every* believer, their normative implications are difficult to reconcile. This is an interpretive conditional, not a demonstrated contradiction without audience and scope arguments.

## Strongest reconciliation reading

Matthew addresses the authority and fulfillment of Israel's scriptures through Jesus' teaching; Galatians addresses Gentile inclusion and the Law's custodial role relative to Christ and the promise. On this reading, the texts need not prescribe mutually exclusive behavior for the same audience. The reading must still explain Matthew 5's unusually strong language about even minor commandments.

## Lexical and historical questions requiring specialist review

- Matthew 5:17: `plēroō` (“fulfill”) may be argued to mean bring to intended fullness, accomplish, or authoritatively embody; avoid selecting one English gloss as a settled lexical proof.
- Galatians 3:19: “added because of transgressions” admits competing explanations of the Law's function.
- Galatians 3:24–25: `paidagōgos` is a guardian/supervisory figure, not simply a modern classroom teacher; the metaphor's social implications need ancient-context evidence.
- Galatians 3:28: unity in Christ should not be equated without argument with abolition of every social structure.
- Distinguish Paul's first-person letter from Matthew's later literary representation of Jesus' speech; do not infer that the historical Jesus read or responded to Galatians.

## Counterexamples that prevent simplistic claims

- **Romans 3:31:** Paul explicitly denies that faith nullifies the Law.
- **Romans 7:12:** Paul calls the Law holy and the commandment holy, just and good.
- **Galatians 5:13–14:** Paul links freedom to service in love and cites neighbor-love as fulfillment of the Law.
- **Matthew 5:21–48:** Matthew's Jesus does not merely repeat commands; he authoritatively intensifies and interprets them.
