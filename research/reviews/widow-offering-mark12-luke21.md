# Widow's offering — Mark 12:38–44 and Luke 20:45–21:4

**Issue:** #4 · **Status:** draft; preliminary narrative-context audit

## Question

Is the widow's gift praised as exemplary devotion, exposed as evidence of exploitative religious structures, or deliberately open to both readings? Jesus observes and describes the gift; the narrative does **not** record him ordering her to give away her last resources.

## Sequence and speaker audit

| Text | Literary action | Interpretive consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mark 12:38–40 | Jesus warns against scribes who consume widows' houses, amid criticism of religious status and exploitation. | This immediate warning is essential context for the offering that follows. |
| Mark 12:41 | Narrator locates Jesus opposite the treasury and describes his observation of contributors. | Scene setting is narration, not a command from Jesus. |
| Mark 12:42 | Narrator describes a poor widow's two small coins. | The exact modern monetary equivalent is not essential to the story's contrast. |
| Mark 12:43–44 | Jesus calls disciples and contrasts her gift from poverty with others' gifts from surplus. | Jesus describes its relative magnitude; the text does not explicitly state “imitate her” or “the temple has exploited her.” |
| Mark 13:1–2 | The narrative turns immediately to the temple and its coming destruction. | This strengthens the possibility of an institutional critique, though proximity does not settle intention. |
| Luke 20:45–47 | Jesus warns against scribes who consume widows' houses. | Luke preserves the adjacent exploitation warning. |
| Luke 21:1–4 | Jesus observes and comments on the poor widow's contribution. | Parallel narrative is close, but must be checked for exact verbal differences. |
| Luke 21:5–6 | Jesus predicts destruction of the temple buildings. | The literary placement invites comparison with Mark's sequence. |

## Competing interpretations

**Exemplary-giving reading:** Jesus explicitly ranks the widow's contribution above the larger gifts because its cost is greater relative to her means. Her trust and wholeheartedness can be understood as the point of the observation. This reading must not turn description into a universal command for poor people to donate everything.

**Exploitation-critical reading:** The widow appears immediately after a denunciation of religious authorities who consume widows' resources, and before the temple's judgment. Her complete loss may function as evidence of a system's failure to protect vulnerable people. This reading must account for Jesus' positive-sounding comparison of the size of her gift.

**Combined reading:** The widow's sincerity and the system's injustice need not be mutually exclusive. This is plausible, but requires textual argument rather than simply blending both readings.

## Comparison constraints for Paul

2 Corinthians 8:8, 8:12–13 and 9:7 emphasize willingness, capacity and avoidance of overburdening contributors. They are **not** a direct response to Mark's or Luke's widow story. Comparing the ethics requires distinguishing a narrative example from instructions for an organized intercommunity collection. Paul also praises the Macedonians' giving amid poverty (2 Corinthians 8:1–5), which prevents an overly simple contrast.
