# Women in the Gospel narratives — discipleship and witness

**Issue:** #5 · **Status:** draft; Gospel-specific comparison

## Narrative inventory

| Passage | Narrated action | Interpretive boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Luke 8:1–3 | Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Susanna and others accompany and support Jesus' mission. | Financial support and accompaniment are explicit; later formal offices are not specified. |
| Luke 10:38–42 | Mary listens to Jesus while Martha attends to hospitality. | Discipleship/learning is prominent; no explicit ordinance for later ordination. |
| John 4:7–42 | Jesus speaks with a Samaritan woman, who tells others about the encounter. | The narrative presents significant agency; historical details and Johannine literary framing remain distinct. |
| Mark 15:40–47 | Women witness the crucifixion and burial from the narrative's vantage. | Mark's witness sequence should not be conflated with later Gospel appearances. |
| Mark 16:1–8 | Women discover the empty tomb and receive a message; the earliest recoverable ending closes with fear and silence. | Do not rely on the disputed longer ending (16:9–20) to make claims about the earliest Markan text. |
| Matthew 28:1–10 | Women receive a message and encounter the risen Jesus, who directs them to inform disciples. | Gospel-specific narrative, not a verbatim transcript independently confirming the other accounts. |
| Luke 24:1–11 | Women report the tomb message, and male disciples initially reject their report. | The narrative itself presents their testimony as contested by listeners. |
| John 20:11–18 | Mary Magdalene encounters Jesus and is told to report to his followers. | A commission to report is explicit; exact correspondence to later clerical offices is not. |

## Comparison with Paul's letters

**Convergence:** These narratives portray women as significant participants and witnesses; Romans 16 and Philippians 4 also portray women as important collaborators.

**Potential tension:** Restrictive instructions in 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 and 1 Timothy 2:11–15 may appear difficult to reconcile with women transmitting authoritative religious messages. However, an eyewitness commission, prophecy, teaching, and oversight are not identical activities; whether restrictions apply to them is a contested inference.

**Methodological asymmetry:** The Gospels are later literary witnesses portraying Jesus' ministry, while undisputed Pauline letters are first-person correspondence. A historical claim about what Jesus *personally prescribed for all later churches* needs more than an episode of Gospel narrative.
