# Mission finance, labor and the right to support

Luke 10:7 and 1 Corinthians 9:14 recognize support for mission workers. Paul attributes the latter instruction to the Lord; the precise transmission relationship to Luke remains unestablished. Retain different emphasis provisionally.

## Claim checks

- First Corinthians 9:4–14 argues for an entitlement to support, not a universal prohibition on paid ministry.
- Verses 15–18 describe Paul's nonexercise of that entitlement. Schellenberg challenges the reconstruction that Corinth actually offered him support and he rejected it.
- Lohr interprets manual labor as identification with poorer members and a means of mitigating patronage obligations. This interpretation presupposes a financial refusal that Schellenberg disputes.
- Schellenberg's discussion of chapters 8–10 links nonexercise of financial rights to accommodation of others' needs. This offers a contextual explanation rather than an absolute financial rule.
- Philippians 4:10–20 is a counterexample to claims that Paul never accepted assistance. It needs separate analysis of gifts and ongoing remuneration.
- First Thessalonians 4:9–12 addresses community livelihood, not necessarily itinerant mission arrangements. Luke's instructions should not be generalized into a ban on productive work.

## Limitations and next checks

Compare the Greek of Luke 10:7 and 1 Corinthians 9:14; investigate the relation to Matthew 10:10 and the transmission of Jesus traditions. Read full competing financial-policy studies and Lukan scholarship. Distinguish gifts, rights, patronage, labor and demands for payment. Modern clergy compensation cannot be settled by these ancient mission arrangements alone. All claims remain draft.
