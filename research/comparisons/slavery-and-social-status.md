## Textual comparison
Jesus' sayings in Mark 10 and Matthew 23 reverse conventional status rankings among disciples. Luke 12 uses household servants and punishment as imagery for accountability. Metaphorical use of slavery is neither an explicit abolition program nor sufficient evidence of endorsement of every historical practice.

First Corinthians 7 discusses enslaved and free persons in relation to calling; the practical force of verse 21 is disputed. Philemon appeals for Onesimus to be received as a beloved brother, but does not unambiguously demand legal manumission. Galatians 3 declares unity across slave/free distinctions without explicitly abolishing the institution.

## Competing interpretations
The texts may undermine status hierarchy through theological equality and mutual service while leaving legal institutions intact. A stronger abolitionist trajectory is an ethical inference requiring argument. Conversely, accommodation of existing social structures must not be confused with a comprehensive moral defense of slavery.

## Modern application
Slavery and human trafficking are violations of human rights. Ancient textual interpretation does not require endorsing those practices. Historical reception, including both proslavery and abolitionist uses, needs a separate evidence-based study.
