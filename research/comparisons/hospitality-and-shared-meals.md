## The guest list and the meal

At a Sabbath dinner Luke's Jesus speaks first to guests seeking prominent places, then to the host who controls admission. He names relatives, friends and wealthy neighbors as people able to return an invitation. His alternative guest list names poor people and people with disabilities. Verse 14 retains the prospect of recompense but locates it at the resurrection. The host is asked to act without a claim on the guests. The subsequent story of refused invitations responds to a fellow diner's remark and has its own set of speakers. [Advice to guests](/passages/lk-14-07-11/#unit-8-11-instruction) · [Address to the host](/passages/lk-14-12-14/#unit-12-14-instruction) · [Banquet story](/passages/lk-14-15-24/#unit-16-24-parable)

Paul begins with an assembly already convened. Some go hungry, others drink to excess, and the people who have little suffer public shame. Paul invokes a received account of Jesus' last meal and calls for a change in how the Corinthians eat together. His remedy concerns the conduct of current participants. He gives no guest list. The text identifies the uneven result much more clearly than the hour when each participant arrived. ## Where the comparison holds

Both texts expose an inequality that a formally shared meal can preserve. A seat at a table might depend on the ability to repay an invitation. Even people who enter the same room may receive very different treatment. Luke challenges a host before the guests arrive. Paul challenges a community over what has already happened at its supper. This supports **different emphasis**. Neither author says that the reform proposed by the other is wrong.

The strongest tension lies in Paul's direction that a hungry person eat at home. Taken alone it can sound like indifference to someone with no food at home. In the same argument Paul reproaches the assembly for humiliating those who have little and tells participants to wait for one another. The instruction can therefore be read as restraining private consumption at the gathering. The passage leaves open who supplied food or whether the change would feed someone outside the assembly.

## What the scholarship can establish

Willi Braun treats the instructions to guests and hosts as a deliberately paired part of Luke's meal narrative. That literary observation helps distinguish the targets of the two sayings. It cannot establish how a particular householder responded. John S. Kloppenborg challenges a familiar reconstruction in which poor laborers arrived late to find the food gone. He argues that the force of the verb *prolambanein* in 1 Corinthians 11:21 is disputed and compares status competition over portions in associations. Neither work schedules nor the seating plan can be reconstructed from Paul's rebuke alone. The chapter summary and the article's abstract and opening discussion were inspected. Critical editions and the comparative inscriptions remain to be checked independently. ## A later community and a modern question

The Didache distinguishes the traveling guest from the teacher who settles and the person who requests money for others. These are later community rules about sustaining hospitality. The passages inspected supply no direct citation of either meal discussed above. [Didache reception study](/research-reviews/didache-ethical-reception/)

A congregation offering a public meal faces two questions the comparison helps distinguish. Can someone attend without an invitation from a donor or an ability to return the favor? Once admitted, can that person eat on the same terms as others? Answers about venue, accessibility and funding require local evidence and decisions about whom the program serves.
