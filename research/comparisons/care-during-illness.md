## The act under comparison

Luke's lawyer asks whom he must count as his neighbor. Jesus ends his story by asking which traveler acted as a neighbor to the wounded man. The lawyer identifies the one who showed mercy, and Jesus directs him to act likewise. The Samaritan changes his plans, treats the injuries, transports the man and arranges care after his own departure. The story ends before the reader learns whether the patient recovers. The action commended is undertaking responsibility for someone in need. [Story and closing exchange](/passages/lk-10-25-37/#unit-30-35-parable)

Paul's immediate problem in Philippians is how to send a colleague home. Epaphroditus nearly died. He is distressed that people at home have heard about his illness, and Paul anticipates their joy at seeing him. Paul describes his colleague's survival as divine mercy. His instruction to the Philippians is to welcome him and to honor people who take such risks in service. The report about recovery and the command about reception answer different questions. ## Agreement and its limits

There is agreement on responding to vulnerability through practical attention. Luke gives the initiative to a stranger. Paul asks for a generous reception within a network of coworkers. The texts give different reasons to act: becoming a neighbor in Luke, affection and recognition of service in Philippians. Paul does not cite the Samaritan story or command every community to reproduce its arrangement with an innkeeper. Read narrowly, his appeal is a commendation of a particular emissary. On that reading the agreement holds at the level of conduct, while the duties owed to strangers require a separate argument.

Matthew adds another motive. In the judgment scene Jesus voices a king who identifies himself with people visited in illness. If the king's brethren are specifically Jesus' vulnerable messengers, the closest Pauline counterpart is precisely a sick emissary like Epaphroditus. If the designation extends to all in need, the Samaritan's care for a stranger supplies a closer ethical comparison. Jerry Ireland's published abstract identifies both readings and argues specifically for cross-cultural missionaries. The abstract establishes his conclusion; evaluating his whole argument requires the article. ## What about a cure?

Paul uses the image of one body suffering together while assigning different gifts to different members. His questions in 1 Corinthians 12:29–30 assume that the same gift does not belong to everyone. In 2 Corinthians 12 he recounts three petitions for relief and a response about power in weakness. He gives no diagnosis of the thorn. Treating it as an identified disease would give the comparison a medical specificity the letter lacks. Neither example cancels the ordinary work of visiting, supporting or welcoming someone.
