## Textual comparison
Matthew 10 restricts the initial mission to Israel rather than Gentile or Samaritan destinations. Luke 9 and 10 narrate commissions involving travel, hospitality, proclamation and healing. Matthew 28 presents a post-resurrection commission to all nations. These narrative stages must not be collapsed into one timeless instruction.

Galatians 2 describes Paul's Gentile mission and its recognition by other leaders, alongside conflict over shared meals. Galatians 3 presents unity in Christ across ethnic and social distinctions.

## Competing interpretations
Paul's mission may extend a universal commission already represented in Matthew's narrative. Alternatively, the historical expansion beyond Israel may represent a significant development in the movement, with later Gospel accounts reflecting that development. The literary sequence of Matthew is not independent proof of the historical chronology of every commission.

## Modern application
Mission, cultural assimilation, religious pluralism and ethnic inclusion should be distinguished. Gentile inclusion does not automatically resolve every modern question about conversion or cultural identity.

## Verification
Compare mission vocabulary, Gospel composition and the historical chronology of Galatians. Examine Acts as a separate narrative witness and consult competing historical scholarship.

## Disputed-authorship contextual witnesses

Ephesians portrays Jewish-Gentile reconciliation; Second Timothy urges proclamation. These are distinct literary contexts and do not independently reconstruct the chronology of Paul's mission. These references are provisional contextual evidence, not an attribution of their claims to the historical Paul. No change to the comparison classification or verification status is warranted by indexing alone.

## Who receives the instruction?

The Twelve receive a destination restriction for a particular journey. Gentile routes and Samaritan towns are expressly excluded. At the other end of Matthew's narrative, the risen Jesus addresses the eleven and expands the destination to all nations. The later command includes baptism and continued teaching. A reader treating the first restriction as permanent encounters tension within Matthew even before bringing Paul into the comparison. [Matthew 10:5–6](/passages/mt-10-05-15/#unit-5-6-instruction) · [Matthew 28:18–20](/teachings/mt-s-28-18-20/)

Paul presents his Gentile mission as underway before the Jerusalem meeting. Titus's case tests whether joining requires circumcision. The handshake recognizes differentiated fields of work, and the request about the poor identifies a shared obligation. Paul does not cite the Galilean commission as his warrant here. In Romans he describes his vocation through priestly imagery and his choice of new territory through scripture. Those are distinct claims about authority, inclusion and strategy. [Galatians 2:3–5](/pauline-teachings/ga-t-02-03-05/) · [Galatians 2:6–9](/pauline-teachings/ga-t-02-06-09/) · [Galatians 2:10](/pauline-teachings/ga-t-02-10-10/) · [Romans 15:14–16](/pauline-teachings/ro-t-15-14-16/) · [Romans 15:17–20](/pauline-teachings/ro-t-15-17-20/)

The draft classification of **extension** applies to the widening mission as represented in these writings. The initial restriction would conflict with a Gentile mission if it bound Paul or all later emissaries. Its named recipients and Matthew's later scene weigh against that reading. Whether Paul knew a corresponding saying of Jesus, and how the historical movement negotiated the transition, remain separate questions. This check used the English chapters of Matthew 10 and 28, Galatians 2 and Romans 15.
