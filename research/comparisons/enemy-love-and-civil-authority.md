## Research question

Does Romans 13 contradict Jesus' teaching on nonretaliation, or does Paul distinguish personal conduct from public authority? This preliminary comparison uses the canonical passages as its starting point, not a completed survey of scholarship.

## Shared teaching

[Matthew 5](https://ebible.org/engwebp/MAT05.htm) presents Jesus rejecting retaliation and commanding love of enemies. [Luke 6](https://ebible.org/engwebp/LUK06.htm) similarly calls for doing good to opponents without expecting reciprocity.

[Romans 12](https://ebible.org/engwebp/ROM12.htm) tells believers to bless persecutors, avoid repaying evil, seek peace where possible and leave vengeance to God. [1 Thessalonians 5](https://ebible.org/engwebp/1TH05.htm) also rejects repayment of evil. These are substantial points of agreement, not merely similar vocabulary.

## The tension in Romans 13

[Romans 13:1–7](https://ebible.org/engwebp/ROM13.htm) presents governing authority as having a punitive function and urges subordination, including payment of taxes. This differs from Jesus' direct instructions to disciples about responding to enemies. The text does not expressly command Christians to exercise that punitive function themselves.

Romans 13:8–10 then returns to love of neighbor and fulfillment of the Law. Reading chapter 13 independently of chapter 12 risks losing the distinction between personal revenge and public authority.

## Competing readings

- **Distinct roles:** Jesus and Paul agree about interpersonal conduct; Paul additionally describes the role of government. This explains the different immediate addressees but leaves open whether a disciple may personally administer coercion.
- **Nonviolent discipleship:** The agreement on nonretaliation constrains Christian participation in violence, even if civil authorities are described as exercising coercion. This requires further argument about the relationship between description and permission.
- **Substantive ethical tension:** A positive account of punitive authority may conflict with a universal interpretation of enemy love. To establish direct contradiction, the comparison must demonstrate that both texts address the same agents, actions and circumstances.

## Modern applications and limitations

Policing, military service, self-defense, criminal sentencing and resistance to unjust government raise distinct questions. Neither the Sermon on the Mount nor Romans 13 supplies a complete modern policy framework. In particular, submission is not automatically identical to unconditional obedience, and refusal of personal vengeance does not by itself settle every question about protection of another person.
