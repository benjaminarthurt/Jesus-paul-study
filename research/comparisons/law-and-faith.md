## Research question and limits

The question is not whether Paul and Matthew use the same vocabulary. Both discuss the Law, but they address different problems. Matthew 5:17–20 attributes to Jesus a strong assertion of continuity with Torah, while Galatians 3 describes the Law as having a supervisory role transformed by the coming of faith. Are these claims incompatible, or do they apply to different people and purposes?

A basic historical constraint matters: Matthew is a later written Gospel account of Jesus' teaching, whereas Galatians is a first-person letter from Paul. The literary and compositional dates must not be confused with the historical order of Jesus' ministry and Paul's mission. The **voice of Jesus in Matthew** is evidence about Matthew's portrayal, not a surviving verbatim recording that independently settles every first-century historical question. See [Novenson, section 5](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/LawintheNewTestament).

**Current assessment: apparent tension, not demonstrated direct contradiction.** This is a draft scholarly synthesis, not an independently reviewed conclusion. The evidence ledger above the article tracks which passages support each reading and which cautions must remain visible.

## 1. Jesus in Matthew: fulfillment without straightforward abolition

In Matthew 5:17–20, Jesus is represented as insisting that he has come not to abolish the Law or the Prophets but to fulfill them. The passage also speaks positively of practicing and teaching commandments, and it places the claim within the opening of the Sermon on the Mount. The next part of Matthew 5 reinterprets specific commandments through examples involving anger, adultery, oaths, retaliation, and enemies.

One possible reading is strong continuity: the commandments remain binding and Jesus deepens their demand, making later release from Torah-observance difficult to reconcile with this passage. Matthew 5:19 especially prevents us from reducing the teaching to a vague approval of scripture.

An alternative does not say the Law was simply repealed. It argues that Matthew's *fulfillment* language emphasizes Jesus inaugurating what Israel's scriptures anticipated, with his authoritative interpretation becoming central to covenantal life. [Bradley Trout's 2015 study](https://doi.org/10.4102/ids.v49i1.1910) presents one explicit kingdom-focused version of this reading. It is a substantial interpretation, not a settled meaning of every commandment.

The tension survives if Matthew is taken to require every specific Torah provision of every Jesus-follower without change. The scope of the audience and of the word *fulfill* remain core disputed questions.

## 2. Paul in Galatians: circumcision, promise, and Law

Galatians 2–5 disputes the idea that Gentile believers must take on circumcision and Torah identity to participate fully in God's promise through Christ. Paul argues that the Law cannot accomplish what faith and the Spirit do in his account of justification, and Galatians 3:23–25 describes an earlier period of custody that has changed.

This is stronger than merely saying people should obey the Law sincerely. Paul treats certain obligations, especially circumcision as a condition for Gentile inclusion, as inconsistent with his gospel as preached to the Galatians.

Yet the **addressees** matter. A Gentile-specific interpretation does not automatically mean Paul commanded Jewish followers of Jesus to discard Torah. [Matthew Thiessen's *Paul and the Gentile Problem* (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780190271756.001.0001) is an important example of that scholarly approach; this project's record currently relies on the publisher's abstract, **not unseen chapters**. Other scholarship emphasizes the force of Paul's language against reliance on the Law more generally.

## 3. An essential counterexample: Paul does not consistently dismiss the Law

Any account claiming Jesus honors the Law while Paul universally rejects it fails to address Romans 3:31, where Paul insists faith does not nullify the Law, and Romans 7:12, where he calls the commandment holy and good. Romans 13:8–10 and Galatians 5:13–14 also link love of neighbor with fulfilling the Law.

These passages do not prove that Paul expects Gentiles to keep every command in the same form. They do mean the contrast is more complicated than an unconditional affirmation on one side and unconditional rejection on the other. Galatians 5 places a warning about circumcision and a summary of the Law through love within the *same chapter*.

Romans 7:1–6 describes a changed relation to the Law, while Romans 8:1–4 speaks of fulfilling the Law's righteous requirement. Romans 10:4 calls Christ the Law's *telos*; English interpreters debate the interplay of endpoint, purpose, and culmination. A rigorous comparison must hold these statements together rather than selectively using just one.

## 4. Mark 7: a food-law test case, not a universal shortcut

Mark 7:1–23 begins with a controversy about handwashing and the tradition of the elders. Jesus' response criticizes practices that undermine obligations toward parents and turns attention to what morally defiles a person.

An influential reading of Mark 7:19 interprets the Greek clause as Mark's narrative explanation that Jesus declared foods clean. This can seem to place the Gospel itself on the side of release from at least some ritual-food distinctions. But grammar and historical context are disputed. [Logan Williams (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0028688523000516) argues that the relevant phrase should be construed within Jesus' own reasoning about food passing through the body, rather than as a declaration abolishing prohibited foods. His proposal should be weighed against the established alternative; it cannot be treated as unchallenged consensus.

Matthew 15 offers related material, with its own wording and narrative emphasis. Nor does a dispute about handwashing automatically mean a direct attack on every dietary prohibition in the Torah.

## 5. Acts 15 and Galatians 2 are different kinds of evidence

Acts 15 narrates a deliberative Jerusalem decision about Gentiles and records conditions imposed on them. Galatians 2 is Paul's account of his meetings and an episode of disagreement over shared meals. Whether the meeting descriptions represent the same event, different meetings, or differently shaped narratives is debated.

The historically sound procedure is to compare these texts without pretending Acts' reported speech is Paul's unmediated testimony. The [American Bible Society's overview of the Jerusalem council](https://www.americanbible.org/engage/bible-resources/articles/the-council-in-jerusalem/) notes differences concerning the meeting's format and its stated outcome. It is a helpful accessible overview, not a substitute for a full specialist study of Acts and Galatians.

## 6. Competing interpretations

### Reading A: substantive theological divergence

Matthew 5 is read as maintaining even minor Torah commandments, while Paul's language in Galatians 3–5 and Romans 7 can describe a change in covenant obligation so substantial that the statements are hard to square as universal rules addressed to the same population. Some scholars have discussed whether Matthew's formulations respond to forms of Pauline Christian teaching. [Novenson's overview](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/LawintheNewTestament) describes this as a possibility while stressing complications.

**Strength:** takes Matthew 5:19 and Paul's strong anti-circumcision argument seriously. **Risk:** can flatten differences between narrative audiences and between Jewish and Gentile obligations, and omit positive Pauline views of the Law.

### Reading B: difference of audience and covenantal function

Matthew's Jesus affirms the authority and purpose of Israel's scriptures; Paul challenges the requirement that Gentiles become Torah-observant Jews, while teaching a moral life shaped by love and the Spirit. Both treat the Law as significant, but in different contexts.

**Strength:** accommodates the Gentile dispute and Romans 3:31, Romans 7:12, and Galatians 5:14. **Risk:** can become an imposed harmony if it never explains why Matthew 5 appears to speak of even the least commandments without a stated Jew/Gentile distinction.

### Reading C: fulfillment and transformation

Jesus' kingdom teaching and Paul's Christ-and-Spirit framework each imply that the coming of Christ changes the Law's role without rendering Torah meaningless. Fulfillment can encompass continuity of authority and transformation of practice.

**Strength:** fits multiple positive and transitional sayings. **Risk:** the breadth of terms such as *fulfill*, *establish*, and *telos* can mask unresolved differences rather than explain them.

## 7. Modern implications, stated cautiously

The ancient texts cannot establish a single direct answer about modern religious liberty, Christian Sabbath practice, dietary restrictions, or whether civil governments should enforce biblical laws. Relevant questions include which rules concern covenant identity, which concern ethical behavior, how Jesus handles disputes about tradition, and how Paul understands Gentile community membership.

Modern Christian traditions answer those questions differently. A future modern-issue dossier must document each tradition's positions using official sources and representative practice, not a generalized claim that all churches agree.

## Provisional conclusion and confidence

**Textual observation:** High confidence that Matthew 5 emphasizes continuity, Galatians 3 argues a changed custodial role, and Paul elsewhere explicitly values the Law.

**Historical reconstruction:** Moderate-to-limited confidence about precisely how different first-century Jewish and Gentile groups interpreted and applied those texts; further specialist scholarship is needed.

**Claim of irreconcilable contradiction:** Not established by this pilot. A real interpretive tension remains if the two teachings are cast as universal and directly equivalent rules, but the texts also contain substantial qualifying context.
