## What can be asked of a sick person?

In John 9 the disciples propose two explanations for a man's blindness: his sin or his parents' sin. Jesus rejects the blame they propose. Luke 9 gives the Twelve authority to heal, yet later a father reports that the disciples could not help his son. Jesus' rebuke in Luke 9:41 addresses the generation; assigning fault to the child or parent goes beyond the text. Paul's questions about different gifts likewise give no basis for requiring every sick person or every member of an assembly to produce a cure. These passages offer no way to assess an individual patient's prognosis. ## Who takes responsibility for care?

In Luke 10 the Samaritan provides transport, pays for lodging and asks another person to continue caring for the injured traveler. In Philippians 2 a sick coworker's recovery brings relief to several people, and Paul asks the recipients to welcome him. The Samaritan story gives no fee schedule. Paul's report identifies no treatment responsible for recovery. Both ask readers to attend to what other people can actually do. [Care during illness](/comparisons/care-during-illness/)

Chapter 36 of the Rule of Benedict shows one way Christians institutionalized that obligation. The house must provide space and a caregiver and adjust ordinary rules for sick members. It explicitly cites Matthew 25. A monastery's internal allocation of resources answers a different historical question from access to a public health system. [Historical study and English edition](/research-reviews/benedict-care-of-sick/)

## Consent when prayer and treatment are both offered

Suppose a congregation offers prayer and transportation to a patient who wants the ride and declines prayer. The command to show mercy supplies a reason to provide the ride. It gives the volunteer no authority to make care contingent on prayer. A clinician considering treatment has a separate obligation. The American Medical Association's ethics opinion describes discussion of the options, burdens, risks and expected benefits, including forgoing treatment, as part of obtaining voluntary authorization. This is professional ethical guidance. The Gospel stories do not supply a modern consent procedure. A person's choice to receive prayer establishes neither clinical effectiveness nor permission for a medical intervention. [AMA opinion and inspection scope](/sources/#source-ama-informed-consent)

## Access and cost

A clinic may offer an appointment that someone cannot afford to attend. Conversely, covering a bill does little if the needed service is unavailable. The World Health Organization's account of universal health coverage assesses access to essential services and protection against financial hardship separately. Its fact sheet reports that 2.1 billion people faced financial hardship from out-of-pocket health spending in **2022**. This is a global estimate, not the number who lacked an appointment or an estimate for a particular town. [WHO fact sheet and inspection scope](/sources/#source-who-health-coverage)

A community deciding between a ride service and a bill-assistance fund needs to learn which obstacle prevents its neighbors from receiving care. A national proposal has to identify who pays, who qualifies and what happens to people whose needs exceed the program's resources. The biblical case for helping someone in distress leaves those design decisions open. [Charity and public welfare](/modern-issues/charity-and-public-welfare/)
