## At the place of execution

In Luke 23:39–43, one condemned man asserts that his own punishment is deserved and Jesus is innocent. Jesus answers the man's request for remembrance with a promise of paradise. The judgment about deserved punishment is the man's. Jesus neither reviews his conviction nor commutes his sentence. Earlier in the same chapter, Pilate declares that he has found no capital offense in Jesus, yet ultimately delivers him to the crowd's demand. The narrative gives readers reason to question an official verdict even as it depicts an official empowered to issue one. [The hearing](/passages/lk-23-13-25/) · [Jesus' reply](/passages/lk-23-32-43/#unit-43-43-promise)

The appeal for forgiveness at Luke 23:34 has disputed textual standing. It should not have to bear the whole weight of a case against execution. John 7:53–8:11 likewise requires a textual qualification. The indexed account preserves the exchange about stoning and the manuscript caution together. ## The sword in Romans

Romans 12 tells believers to leave vengeance to God and to feed an enemy. Romans 13 describes the governing authority as God's servant in punishing wrongdoing. Paul's immediate instructions to the recipients concern submission, conscience and payment of dues. He supplies no list of capital offenses, evidentiary threshold or instruction to an executioner. A reader can reasonably see the sword as acknowledging a ruler's capacity to use lethal force. Moving from that acknowledgment to a requirement to execute for a particular offense takes a further argument. ## Where Christian readings diverge

Augsburg Confession XVI permits a Christian to exercise civil office and administer lawful punishment. Schleitheim VI recognizes a civil use of the sword while denying that coercive office belongs to Christian discipleship. They disagree about **who may administer punishment**. Neither answer by itself settles what a present-day legislature should authorize. The Catholic Catechism's 2018 revision addresses that latter question directly. It acknowledges an earlier permission, appeals to enduring human dignity and protective alternatives, and calls for abolition. ## What would decide the modern argument?

Retribution, public protection and deterrence are different proposed grounds for execution. A claim about deserved punishment must explain why death is proportionate and what weight to give irreversible error. A protective argument must compare execution with the available means of preventing further harm. A deterrence argument must ask about the **additional** effect of execution over a specified alternative sentence. Evidence that punishment in general deters an offense would not settle that comparison.

The US National Research Council's 2012 review found that the homicide studies it assessed could not establish the direction of that additional effect. Its time-bound finding leaves both the magnitude and direction unresolved. It gives no basis for assigning an execution a particular number of deaths prevented. An assessment of current policy would also need a current evidence review and jurisdiction-specific evidence about errors, alternative sentences and the experiences of victims' families.
