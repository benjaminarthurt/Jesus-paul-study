## What can a believer claim about another person's faith?

In John 14 Jesus answers Thomas's question about the way to the Father in a conversation with disciples. In Luke 9 the disputed outsider already acts in Jesus' name and John wants him stopped because he does not follow with the group. The sayings concern access to the Father and the disciples' authority to intervene. Neither scene assigns a magistrate power over worship. In Corinth, Paul expressly limits an assembly's responsibility for judging those outside it while insisting on discipline among its members. ## A tradition that advocates freedom while seeking converts

The Cape Town Commitment, part IIC.6, asks evangelicals to defend the religious freedom of people with whom they disagree. It retains a hope that those people will freely become Christians. This documents a particular evangelical argument for reciprocal protection. The statement's theological claims and a government's reasons for protecting conscience can be assessed separately. Local practice and the views of the people receiving advocacy require other evidence. [Historical reception and citations](/research-reviews/cape-town-hospitality/)

## Can someone decline?

Consider a church running the only available meal service after a family arrives. A conversation about faith at a guest's invitation and a requirement to attend worship before eating create different choices. If declining worship costs the family its meal, apparent participation gives little evidence of religious assent. Serving the meal on the same terms to either family makes the freedom to decline concrete. This judgment draws on the recipient's dependence and the provider's control of an essential resource. Those features of the case supply ethical premises beyond the ancient texts. Whether any particular arrangement violates civil law requires the applicable jurisdiction and facts.
