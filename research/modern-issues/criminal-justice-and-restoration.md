## Three different acts

In Matthew 25 the king identifies himself with people visited in prison. The scene asks what the visitor did for a person in need. It gives no verdict about the prisoner's guilt or the appropriate sentence. In Luke 19, Zacchaeus himself announces giving to the poor and fourfold repayment where he has defrauded someone. Jesus announces salvation for the household. Turning the fourfold figure into a sentencing rule would put the tax collector's words in Jesus' mouth and extend a household encounter into a court. [Matthew 25:34–40](/passages/mt-25-31-46/#unit-34-40-teaching) · [Luke 19:1–10](/passages/lk-19-01-10/)

In 2 Corinthians 2:5–11, Paul judges a sanction imposed by the majority sufficient and urges the assembly to comfort the person and reaffirm its love. He identifies grief as a danger in continuing the sanction. The letter does not specify a modern criminal offense, identify this person conclusively with the offender in 1 Corinthians 5, or assign an injured individual responsibility for arranging a reunion. Romans 13 addresses a different agent, the civil authority. ## A documented institutional reading

In 2000 the US Catholic bishops treated harm to victims, offender responsibility and public protection as concurrent obligations. They supported opportunities for restitution and voluntary victim–offender mediation. They also retained a role for imprisonment when needed for protection. Matthew's prison visitor appears explicitly in the statement. Its program of sentencing reform is the bishops' ethical and institutional judgment. Their historical statistics should be read as evidence about the setting of the statement. [Statement and inspected sections](/sources/#source-usccb-criminal-justice-2000)

## What a conference can establish

A Campbell review reported by Sherman and colleagues synthesized ten randomized trials of face-to-face restorative conferences. Victims and offenders had agreed to meet before random assignment. The authors found a modest average reduction in repeat offending among consenting offenders assigned to conferencing. That result concerns the tested offer of a conference. It cannot tell a court what would happen if an unwilling person were ordered to meet, or establish that forgiveness occurred. Individual experiences and the effects of other restorative programs remain separate questions. [Study design and inspection scope](/sources/#source-sherman-conferencing-2015)

Consider a congregation asked to support a person returning from custody after stealing from another member. It can offer practical help, ask how restitution will be honored, and separately ask the injured person what support they want. A victim can want repayment and decline a meeting. A congregation deciding whom to welcome has a different responsibility from a court determining a sentence or an employer deciding who may handle money. Evaluating those decisions requires evidence about repair, continuing risk, consent and the duties attached to the role.
