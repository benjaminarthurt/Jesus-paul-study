# Gospel of John — preliminary literary inventory and speech-bearing spans

**Status: draft**. The Gospel of John has a different discourse style and literary arrangement from Matthew, Mark and Luke. This inventory catalogues its **21 canonical chapters** into **88 draft literary units** (including two previously indexed research anchors), plus **93 selected speech-bearing spans**. Automated tests assign every chapter-numbered verse to one reference unit **only in the site's chosen World English Bible Protestant edition**; arithmetic does not establish historical or manuscript originality.

## What counts as a saying

The catalog distinguishes **Jesus' narrated speech** from the evangelist's theological prologue, testimony attributed to John the Baptist, interlocutor speech, the narrator's closing purpose statement and later editorial voice. A broad unit labeled `dialogue` can contain both speakers and narration; a selected speech-bearing span can also contain brief narrative framing. All descriptions are **original editorial paraphrases, not verbatim quotations**.

- **John 1:1–18**: the Word/Logos prologue is narrated theology, not direct speech of Jesus. John 1:18 has a significant Greek reading alternative concerning the description of the Son.
- **John 3:16–21**: without ancient quotation marks the precise point at which the Nicodemus dialogue ends is uncertain. We do not present these verses as a securely delimited Jesus quotation.
- **John 3:31–36**: uncertainty also surrounds whether this is an extension of John the Baptist's testimony or a narrator's comment.
- **John 4:7–42 and 20:11–18**: the existing Samaritan-woman and Mary Magdalene study anchors are preserved with their stable citation IDs and classified as dialogue.
- **John 13–17**: footwashing, farewell discourse, extended instructions on love and the Spirit, and prayer have separate literary types; the prayers in John 17 address God.
- **John 20:30–31 and 21:24–25**: narrator/editorial summaries of the book's purpose and testimony, not Jesus' words.

## Manuscript and literary cautions

- **John 5:3b–4**: many early witnesses lack the story of angelic stirring at the pool. The canonical reference range stays indexed, with a visible caution. See the [NET Bible John 5 textual note](https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php?passage=Joh+5%3A4).
- **John 7:53–8:11**: the adulterous-woman story is absent from many early Greek manuscripts and appears in different locations in some later witnesses. It is split into John 7:53 and 8:1–11 only because the local index uses single-chapter IDs. Both sides are explicitly marked `disputed`, and **neither enters the core speech-bearing sayings list**. See the [NET Bible textual discussion](https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php?passage=Joh+7%3A53--8%3A11).
- **John 9:35**: Son of Man / Son of God wording differs in manuscripts; speech detail retains a note.
- **John 20:19–23**: interpreting the forgiveness and retention saying as authority for later institutions is a **theological interpretation question**, not a manuscript-variant label. The record has a caution but is not marked as a variant for the search facet.
- **John 21**: the epilogue's literary relation to the apparent ending at 20:30–31 is debated; no conclusive compositional date is asserted.

## Synoptic relationship

John preserves long, symbolic self-referential discourses that are significantly unlike the short sayings and parables often prominent in the Synoptics. We record a **small conservative list of candidate relationships** (thematic or event-level) only where an explicit, reviewable cross-Gospel reference has been identified. This intentionally leaves most Johannine teachings without a matching Synoptic saying. Neither a candidate link nor the absence of one proves independent tradition or source dependence.

The sectioning uses, as **one guide rather than a conclusive authority**, Daniel B. Wallace's [introduction and outline](https://bible.org/seriespage/gospel-john-introduction-argument-outline). Wallace advocates traditional apostolic authorship; that position is not adopted by the database as settled consensus. Other historical and literary models remain relevant.
