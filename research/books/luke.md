# Gospel of Luke: draft literary units and speech-bearing index

**Review status: draft.** This is an editorial research index, not an independently verified critical-edition or clause-level corpus.

## Coverage and scope

Luke's **24 chapters** now have a first-pass inventory of **143 literary units**, including ten already indexed research passages preserved under their original IDs. A structural validator assigns each canonical verse in the site's reference edition to exactly **one literary unit**, but that arithmetic does **not** establish the best literary segmentation or the antiquity of each verse.

Units distinguish narrator's context and attributed speech, parables, dialogue, action, teaching illustrations and prayer. In Luke 1, Mary's song and Zechariah's song are attributed to those speakers, **not** to Jesus. Luke 2 includes a saying attributed to the young Jesus in the temple. Luke 9:51 marks a major narrative turn toward Jerusalem; the extended journey through Luke 19 is a distinctive literary arrangement, not an additional independent historical eyewitness source. <https://yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-gospel-of-luke/lessons/introduction-to-the-course-4/>

## Selected teachings

The second layer records **112 selected Luke speech-bearing spans** with **113 candidate links** to Matthew, Mark, or John. It retains editorial summaries only, not quotations or a complete verse-by-verse transcription. Some spans include interlocutor speech or framing narration and require clause-level verification.

Examples demanding Gospel-specific handling:

- Luke 6:20–26 sets blessings beside woes against the rich, rather than reproducing Matthew 5's Beatitudes unchanged.
- Luke 10:25–37 narrates the Good Samaritan in response to a legal question; a cross-Gospel analogy is not an identical pericope.
- Luke 10:38–42 depicts Mary listening while Martha serves; no later ecclesiastical appointment is stated.
- Luke 11:2–4 preserves a shorter prayer tradition than Matthew 6:9–13; some transmission histories show harmonizing tendencies.
- Luke 15:11–32 concerns a returning son and his resentful brother. Labels such as 'prodigal son' are interpretation, not a recorded title in the text.
- Luke 16:18 is placed near Law and wealth material, unlike the extended divorce discussions in Mark 10 and Matthew 19.
- Luke 18:1–8 depicts a widow seeking justice and uses the parable to discuss persistence in prayer.
- Luke 19:1–10 portrays Zacchaeus's restitution, which should not be reduced to one universal percentage.
- Luke 22:43–44 and Luke 23:34a have disputed manuscript evidence and are never represented as an undisputed transcript.

## Important textual cautions

- **Luke 10:1,17:** manuscripts preserve a difference between seventy and seventy-two envoys; the registry labels this uncertainty rather than silently choosing one.
- **Luke 22:19–20:** differing cup/bread sequence and presence of phrases in Western witnesses require textual comparison.
- **Luke 22:43–44:** accounts of angelic strengthening and anguished sweat are present in some witnesses but absent from other important early manuscripts. Dirk Jongkind explains conflicting manuscript evidence and divergent Tyndale House vs. Nestle–Aland judgments. <https://tyndalehouse.com/2018/11/15/luke-2243-44-is-the-angel-and-the-sweat-like-drops-of-blood-an-early-addition/>
- **Luke 23:34a:** the prayer for forgiveness appears in many witnesses but is absent from others, including early ones. Dirk Jongkind favors inclusion while acknowledging substantial contrary evidence. <https://tyndalehouse.com/2018/11/15/father-forgive-them-the-variant-in-luke-2334a/>
- **Luke 24:12 and 24:51:** these verses have textual-history questions; the preliminary catalog does not claim to have established their earliest recoverable wording.

The site links each unit to the **World English Bible, Protestant edition (WEBP)** chapter on eBible.org. This edition's translated text is public domain, but its name is trademarked. The research index uses original paraphrases.

## Research priorities

1. Inspect each narrative range against at least two specialist Luke commentaries/synopses, recording the specific references used.
2. Resolve chapter and verse boundaries against the site's exact WEBP edition and identify any verse-numbering divergences.
3. Verify each parallel in its local Mark/Matthew context; distinguish lexical similarity, thematic relation, editorial relocation and possible literary dependence.
4. Consult a critical Greek apparatus for Luke 10:1,17; 11:2–4; 22:19–20; 22:43–44; 23:34; 24:12,51 and other significant variants.
5. Annotate clause-level speech boundaries to distinguish Jesus from narrators, other characters and later textual witnesses.
6. Reassess topic tags and the impact of audience/genre differences.
7. Obtain independent editorial review before promoting any record from **draft** to **source-checked**.
